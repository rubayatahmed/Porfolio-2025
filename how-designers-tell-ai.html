How do designers tell AI how to implement their designs?

A more structured approach might exist rather than staring blankly at the prompt field in Cursor or VS Code.

Introduction

Articulating designs to AI in a logical, machine-understandable natural language has already unlocked a superpower for designers. But there is still friction while translating context. We once thought natural language would be the final abstraction layer that solved everything — yet here we are, still struggling to explain our designs to AI effectively.

Over the last few weeks, I explored how different methods help designers contextualize their designs for AI systems. If we leave aside infrastructure setups like connecting design systems, codebases, and libraries, there are primarily three major context-giving methods:

  1. Prompting
  2. Reference artefacts
  3. MCPs

1. Prompting

Prompting is the clearest way to define intent. It lets a designer explain why a flow exists, how the interaction should behave, and what trade-offs matter. When the product requires nuanced behaviour, prompting becomes essential because a screenshot or design file alone cannot always capture the reasoning behind a decision.

A good prompt can describe not just what the UI looks like, but what the user is trying to accomplish, what happens in edge states, and how the system should respond when the user is confused, rushed, or error-prone. This is especially useful in AI-assisted implementation, because the model needs more than geometry — it needs product context.

2. Reference artefacts

Reference artefacts give AI the visual and behavioural map of the system. This includes screenshots, flow diagrams, existing product examples, or component references. They help orient the model to the broader design language and reduce ambiguity before implementation even starts.

This is especially helpful when there are multiple states or when a designer needs to explain a complex interaction using a broader frame. A good reference artefact is not a substitute for reasoning, but it is often the fastest way to reduce visual uncertainty.

3. MCPs

MCPs are useful because they connect the AI directly to structured sources of truth. In design workflows, a Figma MCP can carry the visual structure of a UI directly into the coding environment. That creates huge leverage because the model does not need to infer the layout from scratch.

However, even with rich design metadata, MCPs still do not fully capture the human reasoning that explains the design. They can tell you what is there; they still need prompting to explain why it is there and what the experience should feel like under different conditions.

Takeaway

The real opportunity is not choosing one perfect method. It is building a layered context model: structured infrastructure, visual references, and explicit product intent together. That is when AI becomes useful as a design collaborator rather than a vague image-to-code tool.

For example, a Figma MCP already carries a rich understanding of the design — layout, spacing, typography, component hierarchy, and other visual structures. But it still doesn’t understand the rationale behind those decisions. That is where prompting becomes important. Prompts are powerful for communicating intent, edge cases, interaction logic, motion and animation behavior, and product reasoning. Similarly, for complex applications with multi-state flows and interconnected interactions, high-level screenshots or flow references can help AI grasp the bigger picture much faster.

Designing for AI is not only about describing the interface. It is about describing the logic, the constraints, and the reasoning behind the decisions. When we do that well, implementation becomes easier, more accurate, and more aligned with the original design intent. The most effective approach is rarely a single method. Designers will likely need to combine prompting, artefacts, MCPs, and infrastructure context together — because each method has its own strengths and weaknesses.
