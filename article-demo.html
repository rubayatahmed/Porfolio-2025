Solving Complex Problems: The Agile Way

How breaking the problem down, staying close to the user, and iterating relentlessly helped our team ship better outcomes faster.

Introduction

In enterprise environments, complexity is the default. You inherit legacy systems, ambiguous requirements, and stakeholders with conflicting priorities. The temptation is to solve everything at once — to produce the perfect spec before a single pixel is placed. After several years of shipping products inside large organisations, I've come to believe the opposite: the fastest path to clarity is through action, not analysis.

This article captures the principles I keep returning to when the problem feels too big to hold in my head. None of them are original — they're borrowed from agile methodology, design thinking, and plain common sense. What matters isn't the framework label; it's the discipline of applying them consistently.

The Right Mindset

Before talking about process, it helps to talk about posture. Complex problems punish rigidity. They reward people who can sit with ambiguity long enough to let a pattern emerge, but not so long that momentum dies.

"The measure of intelligence is the ability to change." — Albert Einstein

In practice this means starting every project by admitting what you don't know. Write those unknowns down — they become your research backlog. Every sprint should retire at least one unknown, either through user research, a prototype test, or a technical spike. If you end a sprint with the same list of uncertainties you started with, something went wrong.

Comfort with being wrong

The best design teams I've worked with treat early-stage concepts as disposable. Wireframes are hypotheses, not commitments. When user feedback disproves a hypothesis, the response should be curiosity, not defensiveness. This sounds obvious written down, but in a room full of stakeholders it's surprisingly hard to say "we were wrong about that, here's what we're trying next."

A Lightweight Framework

Heavyweight frameworks collapse under their own weight. The teams I've seen succeed use a stripped-down loop:

  1. Frame — define the problem in one sentence. If you can't, you don't understand it yet.
  2. Explore — diverge quickly. Sketches, competitor analysis, analogous inspiration. Timebox this aggressively.
  3. Decide — converge. Pick the strongest direction and commit for one iteration.
  4. Build — make it real enough to test. Fidelity should match confidence: low confidence = low fidelity.
  5. Test — put it in front of users. Listen more than you explain.
  6. Learn — document what surprised you. Feed it back into the next Frame.
"Plans are useless, but planning is indispensable." — Dwight D. Eisenhower

The loop takes one to two weeks. At the end of each cycle you should be able to point at something concrete — a validated assumption, a discarded direction, a shippable increment — and say "this is what we learned."

When to break the loop

Not every problem fits a neat iterative cycle. Sometimes you discover mid-sprint that the framing was wrong, and the honest thing to do is restart the loop early. Other times you'll get a clear signal in the first test and can skip straight to building the production version. The framework is a default, not a cage.

In Practice

On the SLB Ride project, the initial brief asked for a "ride-booking dashboard." That framing was too broad. After two rounds of user interviews with travel coordinators we reframed the problem to: how might we help coordinators maximise vehicle utilisation while reducing manual phone calls?

This sharper lens eliminated half the feature backlog immediately. We didn't need a full scheduling calendar; we needed a live tracking board that showed available capacity at a glance. We didn't need an elaborate notification system; we needed inline status indicators that made the current state obvious.

"Simplicity is the ultimate sophistication." — Leonardo da Vinci

The first MVP shipped in six weeks. It was ugly and missing features, but it replaced the whiteboard that travel coordinators had been using for years. The second version, informed by three weeks of real usage data, addressed 80% of the pain points. By the third iteration, adoption had spread to four additional locations without any top-down mandate — people simply preferred it over the old process.

Collaboration across verticals

One unexpected lesson was how much alignment improved when we invited stakeholders into the test sessions. Watching a coordinator struggle with a workflow is more persuasive than any slide deck. It shifted conversations from "I think we should..." to "we saw that users need..." — a subtle but powerful change in how decisions were made.

Key Takeaway

Complex problems don't get solved by complex processes. They get solved by teams that move quickly, stay close to the people they're designing for, and treat every deliverable as a step toward understanding rather than a final answer.

If there's one habit worth building, it's this: after every sprint, ask your team — what did we learn that we didn't know two weeks ago? If the answer is "nothing," change something. If the answer is specific and surprising, you're on the right track.
